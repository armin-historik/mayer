Josef Mayer: patents and technical evidence

Tracing the flat-winding permanent wave through patent specifications, historical apparatus and contemporary documentation.

Josef Mayer’s work is associated with a significant change in the application of heat to wound hair. The patent record provides a dated, technically specific starting point for examining his apparatus. Historical photographs and trade materials help document the wider Mayer Realistic system, but they must be interpreted separately from the patent claims.

US Patent 1,619,794: the primary document

InventorJosef Mayer, Karlsbad
Patent titlePermanent-hair-waving appliance
Earliest stated priority3 April 1924
US application filed19 March 1925
US patent issued1 March 1927
Patent numberUS 1,619,794
SubjectHeating appliance associated with the treatment of flat-wound hair strands

Primary source: United States Patent No. 1,619,794. Consult the original specification and patent drawings. This external record is supplied for independent verification; the historical explanation is presented here.

What does the patent establish?

The patent documents a particular construction for applying heat during permanent waving. Its technical drawings and written description are evidence of what Mayer disclosed in his application. The apparatus is associated with flat-wound hair, in contrast to earlier arrangements commonly illustrated with hair wound around cylindrical rods.

The patent should be read as a description of a claimed appliance, not as proof that every later machine sold under the Mayer Realistic name had exactly the same construction. Establishing that connection requires comparison with dated product catalogues, photographs and other technical records.

Source-critical distinction: A patent priority date is not automatically the date of the first public demonstration, commercial sale or widespread adoption. Likewise, the patent drawing does not by itself establish production numbers or market success.

How the patent relates to flat winding

It is important to distinguish three levels of evidence: the method of winding the hair, the heating appliance described in the patent, and the complete commercial system marketed to professional hairdressers. They are related, but they are not interchangeable. A machine photograph cannot establish the scope of a patent claim, and a patent claim cannot establish how a salon actually used the equipment.
